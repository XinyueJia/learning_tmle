# Chapter 7: How does the EIF describe estimation error and determine efficiency bounds?

The preceding chapters studied how a target changes when its distribution changes. Researchers use a sample to estimate a fixed true target. This chapter connects distributional gradients to estimation error and explains when EIF variance can be called an efficiency lower bound.

## 1. Express mean estimation error exactly as an IF average

Suppose $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$ are independent and identically distributed (iid), with true risk $\mu_0=\mathbb E_0Y$. Subtract the same mean term by term:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\mu-\mu_0&=\frac1n\sum_iY_i-\frac1n\sum_i\mu_0\\
&=\frac1n\sum_i(Y_i-\mu_0).
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** Averaging $n$ identical copies of $\mu_0$ still gives $\mu_0$; the bracket contains the mean IF derived in Chapter 1. Centring at the unknown true mean makes the right side the difference between the estimate and the true mean. Centring instead at the sample mean gives the separate identity $n^{-1}\sum_i(Y_i-\widehat\mu)=0$.

This expansion is exact; general targets usually have an additional remainder. The 5735 RHC patients give current sample risk 1918/5735, while the true population risk remains unknown. We therefore cannot calculate the true estimation error from this sample alone.

## 2. When can an IF average approximate a general estimator’s error?

An estimator is asymptotically linear if it satisfies:

$$
\widehat\psi-\psi_0=\frac1n\sum_i\phi_0(O_i)+r_n,
\quad\mathbb E_0\phi_0=0,\quad
r_n=o_P(n^{-1/2}).
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\phi_0$ is the estimator’s influence function and $\psi_0$ the population target; $o_P(n^{-1/2})$ means convergence in probability to zero after multiplication by $\sqrt n$. Merely having $r_n\to0$ is insufficient: it must be smaller than the leading sampling-error scale $1/\sqrt n$.

For example, multiplying $1/n$ by $\sqrt n$ gives a sequence tending to zero, while multiplying $1/\sqrt n$ by $\sqrt n$ gives 1. Both sequences tend to zero, but only the first is negligible relative to the first-order sampling error. For a specific estimator, this remainder requirement must be proved. Chapter 8 shows how a second-order remainder requires the fitting errors of $Q,g$ to be jointly small enough.

## 3. Why divide variance by n?

When independent mean-zero contributions are added, cross-covariances vanish, so:

$$
\operatorname{Var}_0\left(\frac1n\sum_i\phi_0(O_i)\right)
=\frac1{n^2}\sum_i\operatorname{Var}_0(\phi_0)
=\frac{\operatorname{Var}_0(\phi_0)}n.
$$

> **Notation recap:** Variance scales by squared weights; $n$ terms share the same variance, giving $n/n^2$. The standard error (SE) is the square root of variance, so quadrupling sample size halves the first-order SE.

With finite second moments and suitable conditions, the central limit theorem (CLT) makes $\sqrt n(\widehat\psi-\psi_0)$ converge to a normal distribution with mean zero and variance $\mathbb E_0\phi_0^2$. Sampling dependence, such as clustering by hospital, requires the corresponding variance theory.

## 4. Which estimators does the efficiency lower bound apply to?

A regular estimator must have stable first-order behaviour under local smooth perturbations, excluding rules that perform exceptionally well only at an isolated distribution. For regular asymptotically linear estimators, influence function $\phi$ must represent all the target’s path derivatives. Chapter 6’s projection therefore gives:

$$
\phi=D^*+u,\quad u\perp T,\qquad
\mathbb E\phi^2=\mathbb E(D^*)^2+\mathbb E u^2\ge\mathbb E(D^*)^2.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $D^*$ is the canonical gradient for the specified statistical model; $u$ is an additional component orthogonal to every admissible direction. The norm decomposition becomes a variance decomposition because the functions are centred. The efficiency bound concerns the first-order variance of these regular estimators; finite-sample mean squared error also depends on bias and higher-order terms.

Thus $\operatorname{Var}_0(D^*)/n$ gives the leading variance of estimation error when $\phi$ equals $D^*$ and the remainder is small enough. In real data, $D^*$ is unknown and must be estimated using consistently fitted functions.

## 5. Decompose the variance of the risk difference EIF

In Chapter 4, $D^*=D_X+D_Y$; conditional mean-zero residuals remove the cross-term. For binary $Y$, the conditional variance is $Q(1-Q)$. First average over the two observed treatment arms; one treatment probability cancels from each squared weight:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E[D_Y^2\mid X]&=g\frac{Q_1(1-Q_1)}{g^2}+(1-g)\frac{Q_0(1-Q_0)}{(1-g)^2}\\
&=\frac{Q_1(1-Q_1)}g+\frac{Q_0(1-Q_0)}{1-g}.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** Here $Q_a=Q(a,X)$ and $g=g(X)$; leading factors $g$ and $1-g$ are the actual probabilities of the two arms, and the squared denominators come from $H^2$. Finally averaging over $X$ gives:

$$
V(P)=\operatorname{Var}_P\{Q(1,X)-Q(0,X)\}
+\mathbb E_P\left[\frac{Q(1,X)[1-Q(1,X)]}{g(X)}
+\frac{Q(0,X)[1-Q(0,X)]}{1-g(X)}\right].
$$

> **Notation recap:** $V$ is the variance of a single gradient contribution, in squared probability-difference units. Divide this variance by $n$ to obtain the estimator’s first-order variance. As $g$ approaches a boundary, less information is available under the rare treatment and the reciprocal term may increase; the target itself can remain unchanged.

## 6. Calculate the empirical variance of initial fitted influence-function values

Chapter 4’s initial $Q$ uses only $A$, so its fitted influence-function values $F$ need not have sample average zero. Use the empirical variance definition from the Chapter 0 TMLE overview: subtract the sample average, square, and average over $n=5735$ patients. The denominator is still $n$:

$$
P_n(F-P_nF)^2=P_nF^2-(P_nF)^2.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $P_nf$ averages $f$ values over the same 5735 patients, while $P_nF^2$ squares first and then averages; this differs from averaging first and then squaring. Switching to $D^*$ at the saturated empirical table also changes $Q$ and the target, producing another function. Here $g$ still uses empirical within-sex treatment proportions.
