# Chapter 0: Getting to know the EIF through doubly robust estimation

In the SUPPORT/RHC cohort, 1918 of 5735 patients died within 30 days. Our question goes beyond the overall mortality proportion: **in the same target population, how much does the average 30-day mortality risk differ between receiving right heart catheterisation (RHC) and not receiving RHC?**

Patients who received RHC may have had different baseline characteristics from those who did not. To make this comparison, we need to compare their risks using the same distribution of baseline characteristics. Doubly robust estimation offers a way to make this adjusted comparison.

Below, $W$ denotes baseline characteristics, $A$ indicates RHC (1 for RHC, 0 for no RHC), and $Y$ indicates death within 30 days (1 for death, 0 for survival). Interpreting the adjusted risk difference as an average treatment effect (ATE) additionally requires the identification conditions of consistency, no unmeasured confounding given $W$, and a positive chance of either treatment at each relevant baseline profile. The course demonstrates calculations using real records; the case-study page brings together the [study design, adjustment variables and scope of causal interpretation](case-study.html).

## 1. Use two regressions to predict mortality risk and treatment probability

The two regressions have different response variables. Both can be fitted using familiar logistic regression.

**The outcome regression $Q$ predicts mortality risk.** Fit a regression of $Y$ on $A$ and $W$. After fitting, retain each person’s baseline characteristics and set $A$ in the prediction input first to 1 and then to 0, giving $\widehat Q(1,W)$ and $\widehat Q(0,W)$. Subtract the two predictions for each person, then average over all 5735 patients. This gives the regression standardisation estimate, also called g-computation.

**The treatment model $g$ predicts the probability of receiving RHC.** Fit a regression of $A$ on $W$ to obtain the propensity score $\widehat g(W)$. Inverse probability weighting (IPW) uses this to weight observed outcomes: $1/\widehat g(W)$ in the RHC group and $1/[1-\widehat g(W)]$ in the no-RHC group. Divide each group’s weighted outcome total by the total number of patients, then subtract to estimate a risk difference for the same population.

<div class="dr-intro-models"></div>

The two routes depend on different models: g-computation relies on outcome risk predictions, whereas IPW relies on treatment probability predictions. Since we can learn both from these data, we can try combining them.

## 2. Add weighted residuals to predictions to obtain a doubly robust estimate

Keep the risk difference estimate from the fitted $Q$, then examine the difference between each observed outcome and its prediction under the observed treatment. For patient $i$, this residual is $Y_i-\widehat Q(A_i,W_i)$. Augmented inverse probability weighting (AIPW) uses $g$ to weight residuals: divide an RHC recipient’s residual by their treatment probability, and a non-recipient’s residual by their probability of no treatment, with a negative sign in the risk difference. Finally, add the average of these contributions to the initial estimate.

$$
\widehat\psi_{\mathrm{AIPW}}
=\underbrace{\widehat\psi_{\mathrm{gcomp}}}_{\text{average predicted risk difference}}
+\underbrace{\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n C_i}_{\text{average weighted residual}}.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\widehat\psi$ is the sample estimate of the risk difference; $n=5735$ is the total number of patients and $i$ indexes patient rows. $C_i$ is the signed weighted residual just defined: $(Y_i-\widehat Q(1,W_i))/\widehat g(W_i)$ in the RHC group and $-(Y_i-\widehat Q(0,W_i))/[1-\widehat g(W_i)]$ in the no-RHC group. A hat marks a fitted or sample-calculated quantity; multiplying the risk difference by 100 expresses it in percentage points.

The key property of this combination is **double robustness**: under identification, overlap and the usual regularity conditions for estimation, AIPW consistently estimates the target risk difference if either $Q$ or $g$ is consistently estimated. Here, “consistent” means approaching the true target as the sample size increases.

- **If $Q$ is correct:** conditional on baseline characteristics and treatment, the residual has mean zero. The population average of the weighted residual is therefore also zero, preserving the correct risk predictions.
- **If $g$ is correct:** weighting makes the residuals under both treatments refer to the common baseline population. Their average can make up for the bias in $Q$’s predicted risks.

Either model can therefore provide a route to consistent estimation; if both are incorrect, the result may still be biased. “Mean zero” and “making up for bias” above describe population relationships. The correction in a finite sample will generally still fluctuate.

## 3. The EIF tells us how to construct this correction

Why use precisely these weighted residuals? To answer, we need the influence function (IF). **For a fixed estimation target and reference distribution, an IF maps one observation to a number.** Its value describes, to first order, how the target changes when the distribution gives slightly more weight to records of that kind. For this risk difference, the input is one patient’s $(W,A,Y)$; the output accounts for their outcome residual, treatment probability and predicted risk difference under the two treatments.

The efficient influence function (EIF) is the IF that determines the efficiency bound in a given statistical model. It lets us use the same function for two purposes: **constructing a correction to an estimate and studying its uncertainty.** Under the relevant conditions, its variance determines the attainable asymptotic efficiency bound for estimation error. The following chapters build this rule step by step, starting with the mean.

For the current risk difference, calculate each person’s EIF value using the fitted $Q$, $g$ and initial risk difference, then average over the observed records. The result is exactly the average weighted residual above. This uses the **empirical distribution**: each observed patient record receives weight $1/n$, and we average with these weights. “Empirical” means based on the current sample; in the RHC cohort, $n=5735$, so the death records have total weight $1918/5735$. An EIF has mean zero under the distribution to which it belongs. Here we evaluate the fitted distribution’s EIF on the original patient records, so its average can be non-zero and serve as a first-order correction.

## 4. AIPW and TMLE apply the same rule in different places

**AIPW corrects the final estimate.** Keep the initial $Q$ and $g$, and add the correction just calculated to the g-computation result.

**Targeted maximum likelihood estimation (TMLE) updates $Q$ and then standardises again.** The update changes the estimated conditional mean function. Keep the initial regression coefficients and fitted $g$ fixed, then estimate a new coefficient that adjusts predictions in the direction specified by the EIF. Choose this coefficient to bring the average updated weighted residual close to zero, then calculate the risk difference from the new function’s predictions under both treatments. For a binary outcome, the update takes place on the logit scale, keeping predictions between 0 and 1. Standard TMLE also has double robustness under the relevant conditions.

AIPW corrects the final estimate; TMLE adjusts the risk predictions used to calculate it. The similar results in the figure reflect these particular fits and data; assessing accuracy and confidence intervals also requires examining model fitting and the conditions for inference.

The mean’s IF is $y-\mu$: it takes an outcome $y$ and returns its difference from the reference mean $\mu$. **Why does this difference describe how a distributional change affects the mean?** Chapter 1 derives it from a weighted average; Chapter 8 returns to the same cohort to complete AIPW and TMLE step by step.
