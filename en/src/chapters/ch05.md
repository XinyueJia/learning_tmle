# Chapter 5: Why must we check every admissible direction?

Chapter 4 constructed $D$ using point replacement. Agreement on one path is insufficient: another function might give the same derivative on that path but fail along others. This chapter first covers every independent direction in the RHC joint frequency table, then explains what pathwise differentiability requires in general smooth models.

## 1. How many independent directions are available for eight probabilities?

Eight probabilities sum to 1, so admissible probability changes $h$ satisfy $\sum_{j=1}^8h_j=0$, leaving seven degrees of freedom. Every observation combination has positive probability in the original distribution, allowing the linear path $p_j(t)=p_j+th_j$; for sufficiently small $t$, all probabilities stay positive. The score is $S_j=h_j/p_j$, satisfying $\mathbb E_PS=0$.

Construct one direction for each of the first seven combinations: set its entry to 1, the eighth entry to −1, and all other entries to zero. This gives seven independent $h$ vectors. Every zero-sum $h$ is a linear combination of them. This adds a crucial step beyond checking one point direction: **it covers every admissible direction in the finite model.**

## 2. From paths to directional derivatives

A regular submodel $P_t$ passes through $P$ at $t=0$ and has a square-integrable score $S=\partial_t\log p_t|_0$. Write its directional derivative as:

$$
\dot\Psi_P(S)=\left.\frac d{dt}\Psi(P_t)\right|_0.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $P$ is the reference distribution; $S$ describes the path’s relative direction at its starting point. The quantity $\dot\Psi$ returns one scalar rate of change in that direction.

If, for every admissible $S$,

$$
\dot\Psi_P(S)=\mathbb E_P[D_PS],
$$

then $D$ is a gradient representer. The right-hand side is a probability-weighted inner product: the gradient stays fixed, the direction varies, and each pairing gives a different output. This resembles taking the dot product of a gradient with a direction for a familiar multivariable function, except that the coordinates here are probabilities or functions and the weights come from $P$.

## 3. Decompose a score into contributions from the three mechanisms

Taking logs turns the joint density’s product into a sum. Decompose $S$ as:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_X&=\mathbb E[S\mid X],\\
S_A&=\mathbb E[S\mid A,X]-\mathbb E[S\mid X],\\
S_Y&=S-\mathbb E[S\mid A,X],\\
S&=S_X+S_A+S_Y.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** A conditional expectation first averages within the specified group. Subtraction ensures $\mathbb E[S_A\mid X]=0$ and $\mathbb E[S_Y\mid A,X]=0$. The three terms therefore describe population, treatment and conditional outcome changes respectively, and are pairwise orthogonal under the $P$ inner product.

The conditional probability derivative satisfies $p_Y'=p_YS_Y$, so term-by-term differentiation gives $\dot Q=\sum_y y p_Y S_Y=\mathbb E[Y S_Y\mid a,x]$. Since $Q$ is fixed within the group and $\mathbb E[S_Y\mid a,x]=0$, subtracting $Q$ leaves this unchanged. Thus the conditional mean’s derivative along the $Y$ mechanism is $\dot Q(a,x)=\mathbb E[(Y-Q(a,x))S_Y\mid a,x]$. The outer population derivative is $\mathbb E[(\tau-\psi)S_X]$.

The target derivative is therefore the average over the common baseline population of the two arms’ conditional derivatives, plus the outer term. Multiplying the conditional expectation given $(a,x)$ by $p_X(x)\pi_a(x)$ and dividing by $\pi_a(x)$ restores the expression:

$$
\dot\Psi_P(S)=\mathbb E_P[H_g(Y-Q)S_Y]+\mathbb E_P[(\tau-\psi)S_X].
$$

> **Notation recap:** $H$ remains Chapter 4’s signed inverse treatment probability. There is no $S_A$ term because changing only $g$, while holding $Q$ and $p_X$ fixed, does not change the target.

## 4. Why does the full score give the same derivative?

Let $D_Y=H_g(Y-Q)$ and $D_X=\tau-\psi$. Given $A,X$, $D_Y$ has mean zero, so its pairings with $S_X,S_A$, which depend only on $A,X$, vanish. The term $D_X$ depends only on $X$, while $S_A,S_Y$ have conditional mean zero given $X$, so these cross-pairings also vanish. Expanding all six terms leaves only the previous section’s two terms:

$$
\mathbb E[(D_X+D_Y)(S_X+S_A+S_Y)]
=\mathbb E[D_XS_X]+\mathbb E[D_YS_Y]=\dot\Psi_P(S).
$$

> **Notation recap:** Conditional mean zero removes the cross inner products, leaving only the pairings of $D_X$ with $S_X$ and $D_Y$ with $S_Y$. This verifies that Chapter 4’s $D$ gives the correct derivative for every admissible $S$.

<details><summary>Why do general models also require continuity of the directional derivative?</summary>

In a general $L^2$ model, the directional derivative must also be a continuous linear functional of the score. If $D$ is square-integrable, Cauchy–Schwarz gives $|\mathbb E DS|\le\sqrt{\mathbb E D^2}\sqrt{\mathbb E S^2}$, showing that small directions cannot produce unboundedly amplified pairings. A point mass in a continuous distribution need not be an admissible direction. The definition checks regular paths within the model, rather than arbitrary “distributional changes”. Some targets have no such finite-variance $D$ in a given model and therefore lack the pathwise differentiability used in this course.

</details>
