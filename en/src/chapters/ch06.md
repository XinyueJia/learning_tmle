# Chapter 6: Identify the efficient influence function using tangent spaces and projection

Chapter 5 required the inner product of $D$ with each admissible score to give the correct derivative. Now ask: what determines admissible directions? If a model restricts one mechanism and several functions pass the pairing checks, which gives the efficiency bound? We retain the same RHC joint frequency table and calculate projections using function values and probability weights at each observation combination.

## 1. Calculate inner products and lengths using probability weights

A function on these eight observation combinations can be written $f=(f_1,\ldots,f_8)$, where entry $j$ is its output at combination $j$. Using empirical reference probabilities $p_j=n_j/5735$ from the real cohort, define:

$$
\langle f,h\rangle_P=\sum_{j=1}^8p_jf_jh_j,
\qquad \|f\|_P^2=\sum_jp_jf_j^2.
$$

> **Notation recap:** An inner product multiplies row by row, then averages under $P$; the squared norm is the inner product with itself. Each observation combination has weight $p_j=n_j/5735$. Positive probabilities ensure a non-zero vector has positive squared length.

The space of mean-zero square-integrable functions is denoted $L^2_0(P)$. Here each function has eight values, forming an eight-dimensional vector; mean zero imposes one linear constraint, so this space has dimension seven. General function spaces retain the same weighted-average and length ideas while allowing infinitely many values.

## 2. How do model constraints determine the tangent space?

The tangent space is the closure of admissible scores and their linear combinations. Taking the closure includes limits of sequences of these directions. In finite dimensions, no additional limits are needed for the calculation.

- **The full observational model** allows $p_X,g,Q$ to vary, giving $T=L^2_0(P)$, of dimension seven.
- **This chapter’s hypothetical exercise** fixes $g$ at its current empirical value and allows only $p_X,Q$ to vary, giving a five-dimensional $T$. This exercise lets us compare the effects of model constraints; the course’s observational RHC analysis and Chapter 8 use a model with unknown $g$.

Five basis directions are:

$$
B_X=X-p_X(1),\qquad
B_{xa}=\mathbf1\{X=x,A=a\}[Y-Q(a,x)]\quad(x,a\in\{0,1\}).
$$

> **Notation recap:** The first basis function changes population composition; each of the next four changes one group’s conditional outcome distribution. All five basis functions have mean zero and are pairwise orthogonal: different $(a,x)$ columns cannot be non-zero together, and conditional mean-zero residuals make each orthogonal to $B_X$.

## 3. Why can there be several gradients?

Let $U=A-g(X)$. It has mean zero given $X$ and is orthogonal to every $B$: for $B_X$, average $U$ given $X$ first; for $B_{xa}$, average the residual given $A,X$ first. Both give zero. Thus $D_b=D^*+bU$ pairs with every admissible $S$ exactly as $D^*$ does. Here $b$ is any real number, and $U$ is not an admissible treatment direction in this fixed-$g$ model.

Which components may be added depends on the model. If $g$ is unknown, $U$ is admissible too, and the target’s pure-treatment derivative is zero. The new candidate instead gives $b\mathbb E U^2$, failing for non-zero $b$.

## 4. How does projection select the gradient of smallest norm?

Orthogonal projection finds the function in $T$ closest to candidate $F$. Start with one column $B$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\|F-cB\|^2&=\|F\|^2-2c\langle F,B\rangle+c^2\|B\|^2\\
&=\|F\|^2-\frac{\langle F,B\rangle^2}{\|B\|^2}
+\|B\|^2\left(c-\frac{\langle F,B\rangle}{\|B\|^2}\right)^2.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** Here $c$ is the projection coefficient to choose, and $B$ a non-zero basis column. The final square is non-negative; setting its bracket to zero minimises the expression.

First establish $D^*\in T$: for binary $X$, $D_X=[\tau(1)-\tau(0)]B_X$, and $D_Y=\sum_{x,a}H_g(a,x)B_{xa}$. These are the previous section’s five columns, with a coefficient fixed within each group, so $D^*$ is indeed their linear combination. Together with $U$’s orthogonality to every column, this justifies saying that projection retains $D^*$ and removes $bU$.

When the five columns are orthogonal, calculate $\langle F,B_k\rangle/\|B_k\|^2$ for each, multiply by its $B_k$, and add. A programme can also subtract existing projections column by column to handle a general independent basis. For $F=D^*+bU$, the projection is exactly $D^*$ and residual $bU$ is orthogonal to $T$, hence:

$$
\|D^*+bU\|^2=\|D^*\|^2+2b\langle D^*,U\rangle+b^2\|U\|^2
=\|D^*\|^2+b^2\|U\|^2.
$$

> **Notation recap:** Orthogonality makes the cross-term zero; the remaining term is non-negative. The unique function in $T$ representing the target derivative is the canonical gradient, also called the efficient influence function (EIF). Its “shortest” length uses $P$ weights and connects to variance in Chapter 7.
