# Chapter 3: Use the joint distribution to define a standardised risk difference

Overall mortality does not distinguish why the RHC and no-RHC groups differ. Starting from real cohort counts grouped by sex, treatment and outcome, this chapter identifies each denominator and constructs a standardised target comparing both treatments in the same population.

## 1. Calculate conditional probabilities within a specified group

The original sex field Female is coded $X=0$, and Male is $X=1$. All counts below come from the original CSV and appear in the workbench. Specifically, there are 2543 women, of whom 906 received RHC; 348 of those 906 died. Thus 348 is the number of **deaths among women receiving RHC**. Different denominators answer different questions:

$$
p_X(0)=\frac{2543}{5735},\quad
g(0)=\frac{906}{2543},\quad
Q(1,0)=\frac{348}{906}.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $p_X$ describes population composition, $g$ the RHC probability given $X$, and $Q$ mortality risk given $A,X$. The conditional mean $Q$ equals the within-group average of 0/1 outcomes. Here we use a saturated empirical fit to the current table: a separate mortality risk for each $(a,x)$ group.

## 2. Why can the joint distribution be factorised?

Multiplying the conditional probabilities cancels the successive denominators:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p(x,a,y)&=p(y\mid a,x)p(a\mid x)p(x),\\
p(0,1,1)&=\frac{348}{906}\frac{906}{2543}\frac{2543}{5735}=\frac{348}{5735}.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** The first line factorises into the $Y$, $A$ and $X$ mechanisms; the second is the cell for women receiving RHC who died. Although written $p(0,1,1)$, the coordinates remain ordered $X,A,Y$; the multiplication order does not change their order.

## 3. Why do both arms use the same population weights after standardisation?

Predict risk under each treatment for the same group of people:

$$
\Psi_X(P)=\sum_xp_X(x)\{Q(1,x)-Q(0,x)\}.
$$

> **Notation recap:** Subscript $X$ emphasises that this example adjusts only for sex; both arms use the same population weights $p_X$. The real table gives approximately 0.0736921, or 7.36921 percentage points, a sex-standardised observational association.

The unadjusted difference uses different weights: $\mathbb E[Y\mid A=1]-\mathbb E[Y\mid A=0]$. The first term weights women by 906/2184, the second by 1637/3551. Compare these with the common standardisation weight 2543/5735 to see how the two calculations use population composition.

## 4. What happens if only one mechanism changes?

For fixed $Q$ and $p_X$, the standardisation formula contains no $g$, so changing $g$ alone leaves the target unchanged. **The treatment mechanism $g$ determines how much outcome information each treatment stratum provides, affecting the precision of $Q$ estimation.** For example, if RHC is rare in a stratum, its RHC risk is harder to estimate. The denominators in Chapter 4 and variance in Chapter 7 explain this.

Predict first: if both strata have $g=0.5$, what happens to the standardised and unadjusted differences? Then change one $Q$ and observe both.

<details><summary>Answer and explanation</summary>

With both $g$ values equal to 0.5, the observed arms have the same $X$ composition, given by the original $p_X$. The unadjusted difference therefore equals the standardised difference, which has not changed. Changing $Q$ changes one group’s risk and usually changes both differences. The interface normalises within each conditional group separately; a pure $g$ operation always holds $Q$ and $p_X$ fixed.

</details>

## 5. Fit initial risks using a treatment-only model

Start with a risk model containing only $A$: one mortality proportion per arm, shared across $X$. It deliberately ignores within-arm differences by sex, making the role of targeting easier to see. Estimate the initial $g$ from the observed treatment proportion in each sex group, and $p_X$ from observed counts. The saturated $Q$ and initial fitted $Q$ are different functions; the workbench lists them separately, and subsequent chapters keep them distinct.
