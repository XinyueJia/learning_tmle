# Chapter 4: Derive the EIF by differentiating conditional means

Grouped frequencies from the same RHC cohort have supplied an empirical reference distribution. This chapter asks: **if we increase probability at one observation combination, how does the standardised risk difference change?** We will find inverse probability weights in the changing denominators.

## 1. Write conditional risk as a ratio

Fix treatment arm $a$ and baseline stratum $x$. Let $b=\mathbb E_P[Y\mathbf1\{A=a,X=x\}]$ and $v=P(A=a,X=x)>0$, so $Q(a,x)=b/v$. For point replacement towards observation $o_*=(x_*,a_*,y_*)$, if it belongs to this group:

$$
b_t=(1-t)b+ty_*,\qquad v_t=(1-t)v+t.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $b$ is this group’s contribution to the population event probability, and $v$ is the group’s population probability; both are measured on the probability scale. The value $t$ is the probability path parameter, and $y_*$ the outcome code of the selected observation type.

## 2. Subtract with a common denominator before differentiating

Start without memorising the quotient rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
Q_t-Q&=\frac{b+t(y_*-b)}{v+t(1-v)}-\frac bv\\
&=\frac{v[b+t(y_*-b)]-b[v+t(1-v)]}{v[v+t(1-v)]}\\
&=\frac{vb+vt(y_*-b)-bv-bt(1-v)}{v[v+t(1-v)]}\\
&=\frac{t[vy_*-vb-b+bv]}{v[v+t(1-v)]}\\
&=\frac{t(vy_*-b)}{v[v+t(1-v)]}\\
&=\frac{t(y_*-Q)}{v+t(1-v)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $Q=b/v$ is the fixed original risk; $vb-bv$ and $-vb+bv$ cancel in succession. Finally cancel $v$ between numerator and denominator. Divide by $t$, then let $t$ approach 0, obtaining $(y_*-Q)/v$. Positive $v$ permits cancellation and ensures that the derivative exists near the reference distribution.

If the selected point is outside this group, numerator and denominator both multiply by $1-t$, leaving the ratio exactly unchanged. Therefore:

$$
\dot Q(a,x)=\frac{\mathbf1\{a_*=a,x_*=x\}}{p_X(x)\pi_a(x)}[y_*-Q(a,x)],
\quad\pi_1=g,\ \pi_0=1-g.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\pi_a$ is the conditional probability of actually receiving $a$; residual $y_*-Q$ arises from the numerator change minus the denominator change in the quotient.

## 3. The outer average also changes

Write $\tau(x)=Q(1,x)-Q(0,x)$ and target $\psi=\sum_xp_X(x)\tau(x)$. Begin with the exact difference: $p_t\tau_t-p\tau=(p_t-p)\tau+p(\tau_t-\tau)+(p_t-p)(\tau_t-\tau)$. After dividing by $t$, the first two terms approach $\dot p\tau$ and $p\dot\tau$. The last term is $[(p_t-p)/t](\tau_t-\tau)$: its first factor has a finite limit and its second approaches zero, so the product approaches zero. We are therefore taking the first-order derivative at the starting point.

Along point replacement, the outer weights change at rate $\dot p_X(x)=\mathbf1\{x=x_*\}-p_X(x)$, giving outer term $\tau(x_*)-\psi$. The inner $p_X(x)$ cancels the corresponding denominator in the previous section, leaving only the reciprocal of $\pi_a$. The control risk is subtracted, so the residual term is:

$$
\frac{a_*}{g(x_*)}[y_*-Q(1,x_*)]
-\frac{1-a_*}{1-g(x_*)}[y_*-Q(0,x_*)].
$$

> **Notation recap:** $a_*$ is either 0 or 1, acting as a switch selecting the observed treatment arm. The minus sign comes from the target definition $Q_1-Q_0$; the term’s final sign also depends on the residual.

## 4. Combine the residual and centring terms and check the candidate gradient’s mean

Combine these two terms into a function of the observation $o=(x,a,y)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
D_P(o)&=H_g(a,x)[y-Q(a,x)]+\tau(x)-\psi,\\
H_g(a,x)&=\frac a{g(x)}-\frac{1-a}{1-g(x)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $H$ is the signed inverse probability weight for the observed treatment. $D$ is a candidate gradient for this target, whose values give the change in risk difference per unit path step. Subscript $P$ indicates that $Q,g,\psi$ all come from the same reference distribution.

Given $A,X$, the residual has conditional expectation zero, so the weighted residual also averages to zero. The outer term has mean $\mathbb E\tau-\psi=0$. This establishes centring, but mean zero alone does not prove correct derivatives in every direction; Chapter 5 continues the verification.

The full $D$ also includes the outer term, so calculate both terms to determine its sign.
