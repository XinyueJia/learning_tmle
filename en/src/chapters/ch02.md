# Chapter 2: Describe probability changes with scores and directional derivatives

Chapter 1 fixed an empirical reference distribution from the same real cohort: death probability $r=1918/5735$ and survival probability $c=3817/5735$. We continue studying mean mortality. The question is: **how do the rates of change in the probabilities combine with $D(y)=y-r$ to give the target’s rate of change?**

## 1. Distinguish step size from the direction of probability change

Continue with replacement towards $z=1$. First subtract old from new in each row:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_0(t)-p_0&=(1-t)c-c=c-tc-c=-tc,\\
p_1(t)-p_1&=(1-t)r+t-r=r-tr+t-r=t(1-r)=tc.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $p=(c,r)$ is the fixed original probability vector and $p(t)$ the new one; $t$ is a dimensionless path parameter. Dividing each row’s change by non-zero $t$ gives direction $h=(-c,c)$. The value $t$ determines how far we move; $h$ determines the allocation of change per unit $t$.

The sum-to-one constraint requires $\sum_jh_j=-c+c=0$: increasing probability in one cell requires removing the same total amount from others. Here a vector is simply a column of numbers ordered by row.

## 2. The score records change relative to the original probability

The same absolute change has a different relative size for common and rare categories. Define the path score:

$$
S_j=\frac{h_j}{p_j},\qquad S=(-c/c,c/r)=(-1,c/r).
$$

> **Notation recap:** $h_j$ is the absolute rate of change in row $j$’s probability, $p_j$ its original probability, and $S_j$ the relative rate. A score describes relative change along a path; the propensity score $g$ describes the probability of receiving RHC given baseline characteristics.

The natural logarithm turns a ratio into a difference: $\log[p_j(t)/p_j]=\log p_j(t)-\log p_j$. The derivative of log at positive $p$ is $1/p$. When the probability itself changes along a path, first calculate how $p$ changes with $t$, then multiply by how log changes with $p$. This is the chain rule, giving:

$$
\left.\frac d{dt}\log p_j(t)\right|_0
=\frac1{p_j}\left.\frac d{dt}p_j(t)\right|_0
=\frac{h_j}{p_j}=S_j.
$$

> **Notation recap:** The derivative is evaluated at the original distribution, $t=0$, so the denominator is the original probability $p_j$. The ratio $h_j/p_j$ gives the relative rate at the starting point; the log difference quotient approaches this as $t\to0$.

The score averages to zero under the original probabilities: $\mathbb E_P S=\sum_jp_j(h_j/p_j)=\sum_jh_j=0$. This is exactly the sum-to-one constraint expressed on a relative scale.

## 3. Why does the gradient–score inner product give the derivative?

For any linear path on finite support, $p_j(t)=p_j+th_j$, calculate the mean’s change directly:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\left.\frac d{dt}\sum_jy_j(p_j+th_j)\right|_0&=\sum_jy_jh_j,\\
\sum_jp_jD_jS_j&=\sum_jp_j(y_j-\mu)\frac{h_j}{p_j}\\
&=\sum_j(y_j-\mu)h_j\\
&=\sum_jy_jh_j-\mu\sum_jh_j\\
&=\sum_jy_jh_j-\mu\times0.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $D_j=y_j-\mu$ is the mean IF, and $S$ the current path score. First cancel positive $p_j$; finally use the fact that an admissible direction sums to zero. $\mathbb E_P[DS]$ is an inner product: multiply $D_j$ and $S_j$ in each row, then sum with $P$’s weights.

For point replacement towards $z=1$, the pairing is $c(-r)(-1)+r(c)(c/r)=cr+c^2=c(r+c)=c$, exactly the rate found in Chapter 1. The direction towards 0 has its own $S$; the rule $D$ remains unchanged.

## 4. Include sex and treatment in the observation

Now retain $O=(X,A,Y)$ for each person: $X$ is the original sex code, $A$ early RHC, and $Y$ death. All are 0 or 1, giving $2\times2\times2=8$ possible observation combinations. Counting each combination gives a joint frequency table, with one $(X,A,Y)$ combination per row. Fix a combination $o_*$, with original empirical probability $p_*=n_*/5735$. Multiply every combination’s probability by $1-t$, then add $t$ to the selected one. Thus $S_j=-1+\mathbf1\{j=*\}/p_*$.

The IF for overall mean mortality still uses only $y$. Hence:

$$
\sum_jp_jD_jS_j=-\sum_jp_jD_j+\frac{p_*D_*}{p_*}=0+D_*=y_*-r.
$$

> **Notation recap:** The asterisk marks one selected $(X,A,Y)$ observation type, and $p_*$ is its probability. First use the mean-zero result from Chapter 1 to remove the first term, then cancel $p_*$. We have divided the observations into more detailed categories; the mean target has not changed.

<details><summary>When can a point mass perturb a distribution?</summary>

All observation combinations in this chapter have positive probability. Selecting a point in the support leaves a small two-sided interval in which the new probabilities stay positive. A continuous distribution $P$ usually assigns zero probability to an individual point; adding mass there changes the support or dominating measure and does not automatically define a regular submodel. Chapter 5 checks the gradient identity for all smooth directions admitted by the model. A mean-zero score expresses the sum-to-one constraint; an IF must also give the correct directional derivatives.
