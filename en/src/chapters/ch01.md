# Chapter 1: How do we derive the mean IF from 30-day mortality?

Chapter 0 ended with a question: why is the influence function of the mean $y-\mu$? This chapter derives that rule from the mean itself, then compares it with the familiar logistic regression score.

We continue with the same 5735 RHC cohort patients: 1918 died within 30 days and 3817 survived. For this mean problem, we pool the treatment groups and use only the 30-day outcome. We first calculate the mortality proportion, then change the probability weights of the two outcomes to see how the mean changes. Later, we will use the same approach to derive the IF of the standardised RHC risk difference.

## 0. Define the 30-day mortality outcome

Keep the established coding: original `dth30` values of Yes become $Y=1$, and No become $Y=0$. The values 1 and 0 record vital status at 30 days. If $T$ denotes time from enrolment to death from any cause, in days, we can write this coding as:

$$
Y=\mathbf1\{T\le30\}.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\mathbf1$ is an indicator function: 1 when the condition holds and 0 otherwise. Capital $T$ is time and $Y$ is the outcome code. Lower-case $t$ will later denote the proportion of probability weight replaced: capital $T$ is measured in days, whereas lower-case $t$ is a dimensionless step size for probability change.

The example dates in the figure help us interpret 0 and 1; calculations still use the outcome column in the original CSV. The original study confirmed every patient’s date of death or survival to 180 days, so all 5735 of these 30-day outcomes are known.

## 1. Write the mortality proportion as a probability-weighted average

The value 1918/5735 calculated in Chapter 0 is simply the average of this column of zeros and ones. Grouping patients by outcome lets us multiply each group’s size by its outcome code. Distributing the denominator over the two terms shows how each probability enters the mean. A hat marks a result calculated from the current sample:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\widehat\mu&=\frac{3817\times0+1918\times1}{5735}\\
&=0\times\frac{3817}{5735}+1\times\frac{1918}{5735}\\
&=\frac{1918}{5735}\approx0.334438.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\widehat\mu$ is the sample mortality proportion; 3817 and 1918 are counts, and 5735 is the total. Dividing each count by the denominator gives an empirical probability. Displayed decimals are rounded; the fractions retain exact values.

The figure shows the same calculation: one outcome per patient row forms a vector. Adding all 5735 entries and dividing by 5735 returns a scalar, the mortality proportion. The display previews only some patient rows; the average uses the complete outcome column.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="sample"></div>

We will next change the probability weights, so first fix our starting point. Retain Chapter 0’s empirical distribution $P_n$: each RHC record has weight $1/5735$. In this chapter, use it as the reference distribution $P=P_n$. The survival group therefore has total weight 3817/5735, and the death group 1918/5735.

Below, $\mu$ denotes the mean of this fixed reference; $\widehat\mu$ emphasises an estimate calculated from the sample. Here their numerical values agree, but their roles differ. The true population distribution $P_0$ and its mean remain unknown; for now, we calculate the derivative at a known reference.

Write the death probability as $r$ and the survival probability as $c$. Every subsequent step uses these two exact fractions:

$$
r=\frac{1918}{5735},\quad c=\frac{3817}{5735},\quad r+c=1,\quad\mu=\mathbb E_P[Y]=0c+1r=r.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $r$ is the reference death probability and $c$ the survival probability, found by dividing each count by the total; $\mathbb E_P$ averages with $P$’s weights, and $\mu$ is the result. Every later occurrence of $r$ or $c$ uses these same fractions.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="distribution"></div>

We write this relationship in functional notation: $\Psi(P)=\mathbb E_P[Y]=\mu$. The input consists of the probabilities of the two outcomes; the output is their weighted average. To find the mean’s IF, ask: when the input probabilities change, how much does the output change?

## 2. Choose a specific probability change

To calculate a rate of change, we need a rule that can be varied continuously. First select death, outcome 1: remove 10% of the weight from each category of the original distribution, then put all the removed weight into category 1.

The survival category starts with $c$ and retains $0.9c$; the death category starts with $r$ and retains $0.9r$. Together, we remove $0.1c+0.1r=0.1(c+r)=0.1$ and add it all to the death category. The new weights are therefore $0.9c$ and $0.9r+0.1$. The figure retains the original weights, removed portions and redistributed result so that you can check each step.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="replacement"></div>

The patient records stay fixed; we redistribute the probabilities of outcomes 0 and 1. Write $z$ for the outcome receiving the weight, here $z=1$, and $t$ for the proportion removed and replaced, here $t=0.1$. Varying $t$ continuously gives a whole path of distributions.

This 10% is a replacement proportion. The $0.1r$ removed from the death category returns to that category, so the net increase in death probability is only the $0.1c$ transferred from survival, about 6.66 percentage points. If we denote the probability transferred from survival to death by $\delta$, the two quantities satisfy $\delta=tc$. Distinguishing the replacement proportion from the net transfer tells us which quantity we differentiate with respect to.

Still fixing $z=1$, replace 10% by a general $t$. The new probabilities and their sum are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_0(t)&=(1-t)c,\\
p_1(t)&=(1-t)r+t,\\
p_0(t)+p_1(t)&=(1-t)c+(1-t)r+t\\
&=(1-t)(c+r)+t\\
&=(1-t)\times1+t=1-t+t=1.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $p_0(t),p_1(t)$ are the new probabilities; $c,r$ remain the fixed original probabilities, and $t$ is dimensionless. The last four lines factor out the common term and cancel $-t+t$, verifying that total probability remains 1.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="mixture"></div>

We can condense this calculation into a distribution formula. A distribution placing all probability at $z$ is a point mass, denoted $\delta_z$; it receives all the weight removed from the original distribution. Thus:

$$
P_t=(1-t)P+t\delta_z.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $P_t$ is the perturbed distribution and $P$ the reference distribution; $\delta_z$ concentrates all its weight at the selected outcome. The subscripted $\delta_z$ denotes a distribution; the unsubscripted $\delta$ above denotes an amount of probability transferred. Here $0\le t\le1$ describes an ordinary mixture: retain $1-t$ of the original distribution and place $t$ at the selected outcome.

## 3. Exactly how much does the mean change along this path?

With the new probabilities, recalculate using the same weighted-average rule. Still taking $z=1$, multiply 0 and 1 by their respective new probabilities, then expand the brackets line by line:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Psi(P_t)&=0\times[c(1-t)]+1\times[r(1-t)+t]\\
&=0c-0ct+1r-1rt+1t\\
&=0-0+r-rt+t\\
&=r-rt+t\\
&=r+(-r+1)t\\
&=r+(1-r)t=r+ct.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\Psi(P_t)$ is the new risk; $r$ is the original death probability and $c$ is $1-r$. The second line uses the distributive law; the third evaluates multiplication by 0 and 1. Finally, factor $t$ from $-rt+t$. These terms cancel only partly, leaving $ct$.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="mean-change"></div>

The new mean is now $r+ct$, and the original mean is $r$. Subtract to get the change, then divide by the replacement proportion $t$ to obtain the change per unit replacement proportion. This ratio is the difference quotient:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Psi(P_t)-\Psi(P)&=(r+ct)-r\\
&=(r-r)+ct=ct,\\
\frac{\Psi(P_t)-\Psi(P)}t&=\frac{ct}t=c\quad(t\ne0),\\
\left.\frac{d}{dt}\Psi(P_t)\right|_{t=0}&=c=1-r.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** The first bracketed difference is the change; the difference quotient is “change ÷ step size”. The derivative is the limit of this quotient at the starting point. Here the quotient already equals $c$ for every valid non-zero $t$, so taking the limit does not change its value. The actual change is $ct$; the rate per unit $t$ is $c$.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="rate"></div>

### Does the same rule hold if we choose outcome 0?

So far, we have placed weight in the death category. We could instead place it in the survival category, choosing $z=0$. We want a single formula covering both choices.

Put outcomes 0 and 1 in separate rows, indexed by $j$. Let $y_j$ be the outcome code in that row and $p_j$ its original probability. Retain $(1-t)p_j$ in each row, then add $t$ to the row whose outcome equals $z$. The indicator $\mathbf1\{y_j=z\}$ is 1 in the selected row and 0 in the other, so both new probabilities can be written $(1-t)p_j+t\mathbf1\{y_j=z\}$. Summing over the two rows gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Psi(P_t)&=\sum_j y_j[(1-t)p_j+t\mathbf1\{y_j=z\}]\\
&=\sum_j[y_jp_j-ty_jp_j+ty_j\mathbf1\{y_j=z\}]\\
&=\sum_j y_jp_j-t\sum_j y_jp_j+t\sum_j y_j\mathbf1\{y_j=z\}\\
&=\mu-t\mu+tz\\
&=\mu+t(z-\mu).
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\sum_j$ adds the two rows. The first two sums equal the mean $\mu$; the last indicator selects only the chosen row, giving $z$. Since $t$ is the same in both rows, it can be taken outside the sum. Throughout, $\mu$ is the mean of the fixed reference distribution before perturbation.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="general"></div>

When $z=0$, the death category retains $(1-t)r$, so the net probability transferred from survival to death is $\delta=-tr$; when $z=1$, it is $\delta=tc$. This also explains why the same replacement proportion produces changes of different sizes in the two directions.

The result separates the original mean from its change: $\mu$ is the starting value and $t(z-\mu)$ is the change along the selected path. As before, subtract the original mean and divide by a non-zero step size:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\Psi(P_t)-\Psi(P)
&=[\mu+t(z-\mu)]-\mu\\
&=(\mu-\mu)+t(z-\mu)=t(z-\mu),\\
\frac{\Psi(P_t)-\Psi(P)}{t}
&=\frac{t(z-\mu)}{t}=z-\mu\quad(t\ne0),\\
\lim_{t\to0}\frac{\Psi(P_t)-\Psi(P)}{t}
&=z-\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $P$ and its mean $\mu$ stay fixed; $z$ selects the outcome towards which weight moves, and $t$ controls the replacement proportion. The first two lines cancel the original mean; the third cancels $t$ between numerator and denominator when $t$ is non-zero. The quotient no longer depends on $t$, so its limit in the fourth line is the same number. At $t=0$ the distributions coincide and the quotient $0/0$ is undefined; the derivative is the limit of the quotients as the step approaches zero.

## 4. How does the rate of change become an influence function?

We now have two answers to check: replacement towards 0 gives rate $0-\mu=-r$, and replacement towards 1 gives rate $1-\mu=c$. Only the selected outcome $z$ changed; the original distribution $P$ and mean $\mu$ stayed the same.

The influence function (IF) collects these rates into one rule: take an outcome and return the mean’s rate of change when probability weight moves towards it. The input $z$ is 0 or 1, and the output $z-\mu$ is a real number:

$$
D(z;P)=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\Psi((1-t)P+t\delta_z)\right|_{t=0}=z-\mu.
$$

> **Notation recap:** $D(z;P)$ takes a specific outcome $z$ and returns the corresponding rate of change. The $P$ after the semicolon identifies the distribution supplying the reference mean $\mu$. We can also write this rule as $D^{\mathrm{mean}}_P(z)$: either a subscript or a semicolon marks the fixed reference. We differentiate with respect to replacement proportion $t$, holding $z$ fixed.

The derivatives determine both possible function values: 0 maps to $-\mu$, and 1 to $1-\mu$. Together these give “input minus reference mean”. The figure links the derivative results to the function’s two inputs.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="rate-function"></div>

In the derivation, $z$ emphasises the outcome selected as the replacement direction. When applying the function to a known record, we usually write its observed outcome as $y$. Thus $D(y;P)=y-\mu$ is precisely the rule used in Chapter 0; changing the input letter does not change the calculation. Input 0 gives about −0.3344, and input 1 about +0.6656. The figure places both inputs beside their outputs.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="influence"></div>

Once the rule is fixed, we can discuss the probability of each output. When drawing a record from the fixed reference distribution $P$, capital $Y$ denotes its as-yet unknown outcome. Substituting it into the same function makes $D(Y;P)=Y-\mu$ a random variable. It equals $c$ with death probability 1918/5735, and $-r$ with survival probability 3817/5735. The figure maps outcome probabilities directly to output probabilities.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="random-influence"></div>

### Why do two non-zero outputs average to zero?

Now average all possible outputs according to their probabilities. Survival gives the negative value $-r$, and death the positive value $c$; their magnitudes and probabilities differ. Multiply each output by its reference probability, then add:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_P[D(Y;P)]&=c(0-r)+r(1-r)\\
&=0c-cr+r-r^2\\
&=-cr+r-r^2\\
&=r-r(c+r)\\
&=r-r\times1=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $c,r$ are the two row weights, and the brackets contain the two influence values. After weighting by their respective probabilities, positive and negative contributions cancel exactly because $c+r=1$. Each outcome keeps its own influence value; zero is their weighted average.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="centering"></div>

The positive and negative parts cancel because each outcome has had the same mean subtracted, calculated with these same probabilities. Writing the two categories as a sum makes the reason clearer:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\mathbb E_P[Y-\mu]&=\sum_jp_j(y_j-\mu)\\
&=\sum_jp_jy_j-\sum_jp_j\mu\\
&=\mu-\mu\sum_jp_j\\
&=\mu-\mu\times1=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\mu$ is fixed across rows, so it comes outside the sum. The first term is $\mu$ by the definition of the mean, and the probabilities sum to 1. Centring places the function in the mean-zero space; the next chapter explains its connection to the constraint that probabilities sum to 1.

<div class="course-visual" data-visual="centering-proof"></div>

We must distinguish two ways of averaging the same function. We constructed the rule using $P$, then averaged using $P$’s probabilities, so the centre matched exactly. If we keep the old rule $D(Y;P)=Y-\mu$ but average with $P_t$’s probabilities, we get $\mathbb E_{P_t}D=\Psi(P_t)-\mu=t(z-\mu)$. What remains is the difference between the new and old means.

To obtain mean zero under the new distribution, also change the rule’s centre to the new mean, using $Y-\Psi(P_t)$. The figure first fixes the old function, then updates the averaging weights, and finally updates the function’s centre, showing which object changes each time.

### How does the same residual connect to the logistic regression score?

Probability replacement has given us the mean IF. Now consider an intercept-only logistic model and derive the same residual by differentiating the log-likelihood. For a binary outcome $Y$, one probability $\mu$ determines the entire Bernoulli distribution. Using an intercept $\beta=\operatorname{logit}(\mu)$ is a different parametrisation of the same family of distributions. Here $0<\mu<1$, so both outcomes have positive probability.

Fix one record’s outcome $y$, write its probability and take its logarithm. The derivative of this log-likelihood with respect to the intercept is the single-observation score:

$$
\begin{aligned}
p_\beta(y)&=\mu^y(1-\mu)^{1-y},\qquad \mu=\frac{e^\beta}{1+e^\beta},\\
\ell(\beta;y)&=y\log\mu+(1-y)\log(1-\mu)\\
&=y\{\beta-\log(1+e^\beta)\}+(1-y)\{-\log(1+e^\beta)\}\\
&=y\beta-\{y+(1-y)\}\log(1+e^\beta)\\
&=y\beta-\log(1+e^\beta),\\
s_\beta(y)&=\frac{\partial\ell(\beta;y)}{\partial\beta}
=y-\frac{e^\beta}{1+e^\beta}=y-\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $y$ is fixed at 0 or 1; $\beta$ is the intercept being varied, and $\mu$ changes with $\beta$ through expit. $p_\beta(y)$ gives the probability of this outcome; $\ell$ is its natural logarithm, and $s_\beta$ is one record’s derivative contribution. The third line substitutes the logit parametrisation into both terms; the fifth combines $y+(1-y)=1$. This derivative is with respect to $\beta$, whereas the earlier IF derivation differentiated with respect to replacement proportion $t$.

Thus, at the current reference, both rules return about +0.6656 for a patient who died and about −0.3344 for a survivor. For the score, the sign tells us how increasing the intercept changes that record’s log-likelihood. For the mean IF, it tells us how adding weight towards this outcome changes the death probability.

How far does this equality extend? First sum the scores. Below, $\widehat\beta$ is the intercept-only maximum likelihood estimate and $\widehat\mu=\operatorname{expit}(\widehat\beta)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
0&=\sum_{i=1}^n s_{\widehat\beta}(y_i)
=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\widehat\mu)\\
&=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i-n\widehat\mu,\\
n\widehat\mu&=\sum_{i=1}^n y_i,\qquad
\widehat\mu=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n y_i=\frac{1918}{5735}.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $n$ is 5735 patients, and $\sum_i y_i$ is 1918 deaths. Hats mark results fitted from these data. We solve for a total score of zero; each record still contributes its own positive or negative value. The fitted probability equals the sample mean, so the corresponding mean IF also has empirical average zero. The estimation error relative to the unknown true mean $\mu_0$ remains $\widehat\mu-\mu_0$.

### Change the target to distinguish the roles of scores and IFs

Keep the same Bernoulli distribution and logistic score, but suppose we now want to report the logit of death probability, $\beta$. We already calculated the death probability after replacement, $\mu_t=\mu+t(z-\mu)$. Substitute this into the logit and differentiate using the chain rule:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\beta_t&=\log\mu_t-\log(1-\mu_t),\\
D^{\mathrm{logit}}_P(z)
&=\left.\frac{d\beta_t}{dt}\right|_{t=0}\\
&=\left\{\frac1\mu+\frac1{1-\mu}\right\}(z-\mu)\\
&=\frac{(1-\mu)+\mu}{\mu(1-\mu)}(z-\mu)
=\frac{z-\mu}{\mu(1-\mu)}.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $\mu_t$ is the death probability after replacement, and $\beta_t$ its logit. $D^{\mathrm{logit}}_P$ denotes the IF for the new target, logit death probability; differentiation is still with respect to $t$. The two minus signs cancel when differentiating $-\log(1-\mu_t)$. The factor $z-\mu$ comes from the already established $d\mu_t/dt$; the preceding bracket converts probability change to logit change.

We can now see that one score can correspond to IFs for different targets. The mean target returns $z-\mu$; the logit target additionally divides by $\mu(1-\mu)$. This divisor is also visible in the likelihood: it is the model’s single-observation Fisher information, denoted $I_\beta$, describing the curvature of the log-likelihood in $\beta$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\mu}{d\beta}
&=\frac{e^\beta(1+e^\beta)-e^{2\beta}}{(1+e^\beta)^2}
=\frac{e^\beta}{(1+e^\beta)^2}=\mu(1-\mu),\\
-\frac{\partial s_\beta(y)}{\partial\beta}&=\mu(1-\mu),\qquad
I_\beta=\mathbb E_P\!\left[-\frac{\partial s_\beta(Y)}{\partial\beta}\right]=\mu(1-\mu),\\
D^{\mathrm{logit}}_P(y)&=I_\beta^{-1}s_\beta(y),\\
D^{\mathrm{mean}}_P(y)&=\frac{d\mu}{d\beta}\,D^{\mathrm{logit}}_P(y)
=\mu(1-\mu)\frac{y-\mu}{\mu(1-\mu)}=y-\mu.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $I_\beta$ is information from one observation, and $I_\beta^{-1}$ is its reciprocal in this one-parameter example. The first line uses the quotient rule; the second differentiates the score and changes its sign. The result does not depend on $y$, so taking its expectation leaves it unchanged. The final two lines first convert to the logit target, then multiply by the expit derivative to return to the probability target; the scale factors cancel. These relationships are derived directly in the present Bernoulli model; efficiency in general models is left to Chapters 6–7.

### From a regression parameter score to a distributional path score

Finally, write “change the intercept slightly” as a distributional change. Take another path: the intercept changes from $\beta$ to $\beta+t$, so death probability becomes $\mu_t=\operatorname{expit}(\beta+t)$. This paragraph reuses $t$ for an **increment on the logit scale**; this path differs from the earlier replacement path.

The path score is the derivative of log probability along this path. Since $d(\beta+t)/dt=1$, it equals the intercept score above. The target’s rate of change is the expit derivative:

$$
\begin{aligned}
S_{\mathrm{logit}}(y)
&=\left.\frac{d}{dt}\log p_{\beta+t}(y)\right|_{t=0}
=s_\beta(y)=y-\mu,\\
\left.\frac{d\mu_t}{dt}\right|_{t=0}&=\mu(1-\mu),\\
\mathbb E_P[D^{\mathrm{mean}}_P(Y)S_{\mathrm{logit}}(Y)]
&=(1-\mu)(-\mu)(-\mu)+\mu(1-\mu)(1-\mu)\\
&=\mu(1-\mu)\{\mu+(1-\mu)\}
=\mu(1-\mu).
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $S$ describes this specified logit path, and $D$ describes the mean target. Expectation uses the fixed $P$ probabilities: weight $1-\mu$ for $Y=0$ and weight $\mu$ for $Y=1$. Multiplying the two functions pointwise and averaging gives the target’s rate of change along this path. This is a concrete calculation of “pairing a gradient with a direction” in a familiar regression model.

Three ideas now connect: changing a regression parameter creates a path of distributions; the score describes that path; and multiplying the IF by the score and averaging under the reference distribution gives the target’s path derivative. We have checked the logit path. The next chapter returns to probability replacement, derives its score from its own probability formula and checks the same pairing.

## 6. What changes if we report 30-day survival probability?

The experiment also showed survival probability. It sums to 1 with death probability, so any increase in death probability produces an equal decrease in survival probability. Write 30-day survival probability as $S(30)=1-\mu$. Along the same path, new survival minus old survival is $[1-\Psi(P_t)]-[1-\mu]=-t(z-\mu)$, so its IF is $-(z-\mu)$. Here $S(30)$ means survival probability at day 30. The next chapter also uses $S$ for a path score; its arguments and surrounding formula distinguish the two uses.

<details><summary>Why is there no approximation remainder for the mean? Can t be negative?</summary>

Revisit the expansion in §3: outcome values stay fixed, and changes enter only through their probability weights, each linear in $t$. Their weighted sum therefore contains only a constant and a term linear in $t$, making $\Psi(P_t)=\mu+t(z-\mu)$ exact. This is linearity of the mean in probability weights: change equals step size times rate exactly. Later conditional means contain quotients whose denominators also change along the path, requiring us to distinguish first-order terms from remainders.

This chapter’s outcomes are only 0 and 1, with strictly positive reference probabilities $c$ and $r$, so the point-mass replacement path permits differentiation near the starting point. With $z=1$, both probabilities are strictly positive for $-r/c<t<1$; including endpoints permits zero probabilities. Negative $t$ adjusts probabilities in the opposite direction; the ordinary “remove and replace” mixture interpretation applies for $t$ between 0 and 1.

For similar paths in continuous distributions, regular submodel conditions need separate checking: adding a point mass may change the support and density properties. Chapter 5 uses smooth score directions allowed by the model to express and check derivatives.

## Next: describe probability changes with directions and scores

The rule $y-\mu$ used in Chapter 0 now has a derivation: place a small proportion of distributional weight at outcome $y$, and the mean changes by $y-\mu$ per unit replacement proportion. For fixed $P$, the influence values of the two outcomes average to zero using $P$’s probabilities. If another distribution supplies the averaging weights, check the function’s centre again. Linearity of the mean makes these changes exact.

The next chapter returns to the same replacement path and separates two aspects of probability change: how much does a category’s probability change, and how large is that change relative to its original probability? As with the logit path, we will derive its score and pair it with the mean IF, gradually moving towards more general distributional directions.
