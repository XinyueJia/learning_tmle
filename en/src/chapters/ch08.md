## 1. Why does a first-order expansion suggest bias correction?

Let $P_0$ be the unknown true observed-data distribution, with $\psi_0=\Psi(P_0)$. Hats denote sample fits, and $P_nf=n^{-1}\sum_i f(O_i)$ is the empirical average. The initial plug-in estimate is:

$$
\widehat\psi_{\rm plug}=\frac1n\sum_i[\widehat Q(1,W_i)-\widehat Q(0,W_i)].
$$

> **Notation recap:** Hold each person’s $W$ fixed and predict under $A=1$ and $A=0$. Average both sets of predictions over the same $n$ people, retaining identical baseline composition. The function $Q$ is fitted mortality probability and has not yet been updated.

The distributional gradient suggests that, if the initial fit leaves residuals in the target direction, the fitted EIF’s sample average can provide a first-order correction. The centring term cancels exactly when averaged over the same empirical $W$, leaving:

$$
\widehat\psi_{\rm AIPW}=\widehat\psi_{\rm plug}
+\frac1n\sum_i\widehat H_i[Y_i-\widehat Q(A_i,W_i)],
\quad \widehat H_i=\frac{A_i}{\widehat g(W_i)}-\frac{1-A_i}{1-\widehat g(W_i)}.
$$

> **Notation recap:** AIPW means augmented inverse probability weighting, also called a one-step correction. The weight $H$ comes from Chapter 4’s quotient; $Y-Q$ is the residual under the observed treatment. It corrects the final number while leaving initial $Q$ unchanged.

## 2. What else does valid inference require beyond double robustness?

First treat the fitted functions as given. Let $e_a=\widehat Q_a-Q_{0a}$ and $\delta g=\widehat g-g_0$. Given $W$, the weighted residual has mean $-g_0e_1/\widehat g+(1-g_0)e_0/(1-\widehat g)$. The initial conditional difference exceeds the true difference by $e_1-e_0$. Adding and collecting coefficients gives:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B&=\mathbb E_0\left[e_1-e_0-\frac{g_0}{\widehat g}e_1+\frac{1-g_0}{1-\widehat g}e_0\right]\\
&=\mathbb E_0\left[\left(1-\frac{g_0}{\widehat g}\right)e_1+
\left(\frac{1-g_0}{1-\widehat g}-1\right)e_0\right]\\
&=\mathbb E_0\left[(\widehat g-g_0)
\left\{\frac{e_1}{\widehat g}+\frac{e_0}{1-\widehat g}\right\}\right].
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $B$ is defined as the population-averaged AIPW expression minus the true target, involving unknown $P_0$. If $Q$ is correct in both arms, or $g$ is correct, the product is zero.

Double robustness follows from this global product identity. Neyman orthogonality describes a local derivative near the true functions: small nuisance-function errors have no first-order effect. Valid statistical inference additionally requires a remainder smaller than $n^{-1/2}$, a fitted influence function approaching the true EIF, and appropriate sampling and empirical-process conditions.

For example, if both error norms shrink only at rate $n^{-1/4}$, their product is $n^{-1/2}$, which is not negligible on the required scale. At least one error must shrink faster for this rate argument to establish the requirement. Cross-fitting helps control empirical-process terms caused by training dependence. Clinical confounding and original-cohort selection are addressed through study design and identification assumptions. This chapter’s main-effects regressions demonstrate the full calculation; the chosen fitting methods still require justification of their asymptotic conditions.

## 3. Why does TMLE change Q rather than directly adding a number?

TMLE retains the same target rule and updates initial risks in the direction identified by the EIF. Define logit (log odds) and its inverse expit:

$$
\operatorname{logit}(q)=\log\frac q{1-q},\quad
\operatorname{expit}(u)=\frac1{1+e^{-u}},\quad
Q_\epsilon(a,w)=\operatorname{expit}\left[\operatorname{logit}\widehat Q(a,w)+\epsilon H_{\widehat g}(a,w)\right].
$$

> **Notation recap:** The initial logit is a fixed offset with coefficient 1. The clever covariate $H$ comes from the EIF’s residual weight; $\epsilon$ is the only new parameter estimated in this update. For $a=0$, $H$ is negative; for $a=1$, it is positive. Both predictions must be updated.

During updating, $g$, the empirical $W$ distribution and original patient records stay fixed; only predicted risks change. Expit maps finite real numbers into the interval between 0 and 1. If an operation exceeds the representable numerical range, the programme reports a numerical boundary error.

## 4. Derive the logistic update score with the chain rule

The linear predictor for the observed treatment is $\eta_i=\operatorname{logit}\widehat Q(A_i,W_i)+\epsilon\widehat H_i$; write $q_i=\operatorname{expit}(\eta_i)$. The average log-likelihood for a binary outcome is:

$$
\ell_n(\epsilon)=\frac1n\sum_i[Y_i\log q_i+(1-Y_i)\log(1-q_i)].
$$

> **Notation recap:** $q_i$ selects only the prediction under each person’s observed treatment $A$; $Y=1$ uses $\log q$ and $Y=0$ uses $\log(1-q)$. The parameter $\epsilon$ changes $q$ through $\eta$.

First note $dq_i/d\eta_i=q_i(1-q_i)$ and $d\eta_i/d\epsilon=H_i$, then multiply through the layers:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d\ell_n}{d\epsilon}
&=\frac1n\sum_i\left[\frac{Y_i}{q_i}-\frac{1-Y_i}{1-q_i}\right]q_i(1-q_i)H_i\\
&=\frac1n\sum_i[Y_i(1-q_i)-(1-Y_i)q_i]H_i\\
&=\frac1n\sum_i[Y_i-Y_iq_i-q_i+Y_iq_i]H_i\\
&=\frac1n\sum_iH_i(Y_i-q_i)=s_n(\epsilon),\\
\frac{ds_n}{d\epsilon}&=-\frac1n\sum_iH_i^2q_i(1-q_i)<0.
\end{aligned}
$$

> **Notation recap:** $s_n$ is the function in the likelihood score equation, giving the likelihood’s slope in $\epsilon$. The middle terms $-Y_iq_i+Y_iq_i$ cancel, leaving a residual. The negative slope ensures uniqueness whenever a finite root exists; that root also maximises the likelihood along this one-dimensional path.

If $s_n(0)>0$, increase $\epsilon$ to lower the score. Bisection first finds endpoints with opposite signs and tests their midpoint. A positive score retains the interval to the midpoint’s right; a negative score retains the interval to its left. Both the sex-stratified and multivariable experiments permit step-by-step manual operation; automatic root-finding runs only after you click.

## 5. First calculate TMLE manually with real grouped counts

The four groups’ offsets are the logits of their observed treatment arms’ initial mortality proportions; $g$ is estimated from within-sex counts. Each group summarises its score using its patient count $n_{xa}$ and death count $m_{xa}$:

$$
s_n(\epsilon)=\frac1{5735}\sum_{x,a}H(a,x)[m_{xa}-n_{xa}Q_\epsilon(a,x)].
$$

> **Notation recap:** $m$ is the observed death count and $n_{xa}Q$ the predicted death count. Subtract and multiply by $H$; dividing by 5735 gives a per-record average.

Predict the score’s sign, enter $\epsilon$, and perform at least five manual bisections before opening the answer. Once the root is found, standardise again: $\widehat\psi_T=\sum_x\widehat p_X(x)[Q^*(1,x)-Q^*(0,x)]$, where $Q^*=Q_{\widehat\epsilon}$.

## 7. Why does the empirical EIF equation become zero? What errors remain?

The plug-in definition ensures $P_n[Q^*(1,W)-Q^*(0,W)-\widehat\psi_T]=0$; targeting ensures $P_n[H(Y-Q^*)]\approx0$. Adding gives an approximately zero sample average of the full fitted EIF; individual records retain their residuals.

Using the bias identity for the fitted functions:

$$
\widehat\psi_T-\psi_0=(P_n-P_0)\widehat D^*+B(Q^*,\widehat g)-P_n\widehat D^*.
$$

> **Notation recap:** The first term is the difference between empirical and population averages; the second remains the preceding error product, now with updated $Q$. The last term is zero and may be omitted only for an exact solution; numerical solutions retain their root-finding tolerance. The second term’s size depends jointly on the fitting errors of $Q,g$. Replacing the first term by $(P_n-P_0)D_0^*$ also requires controlling the empirical process of the difference between fitted and true functions.
